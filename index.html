Chapter 1

Fundamentals of Gemology

Gemology is the scientific study of gemstones — natural, treated, and synthetic. It covers their chemical composition, crystal structure, and optical behavior.

What Is a Gemstone?

A mineral, rock, or organic material cut and polished for use in jewelry or collection. Defined by beauty, durability, and rarity.

Formation

Most gems form deep in Earth's crust or mantle under high heat and pressure over millions of years. Some, like amber and pearl, are organic.

Crystal Systems

Gems crystallize in 7 systems: cubic, hexagonal, trigonal, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, and triclinic. This governs shape and optical properties.

Mineral Gems

Inorganic, naturally occurring crystalline solids. Examples: diamond, ruby, emerald, sapphire.

Organic Gems

Produced by living organisms. Examples: pearl (mollusks), amber (tree resin), coral, jet.

Synthetic Gems

Lab-created with identical chemistry and structure to natural counterparts. Same properties, different origin.

Chapter 2

Gem Properties

The measurable physical and optical characteristics used to evaluate and identify gemstones.

The 4 Cs of Gem Grading

C

Color

Evaluated by hue (spectral color), tone (lightness/darkness), and saturation (intensity). The most important factor for colored stones.

C

Clarity

Presence or absence of inclusions (internal) and blemishes (surface). Graded from Flawless to Included. Some inclusions actually increase value (e.g., star sapphire).

C

Cut

The quality of faceting — proportions, symmetry, and polish. Determines how light interacts with the stone. Not to be confused with shape.

C

Carat Weight

1 carat = 0.2 grams. Price per carat increases non-linearly — a 2ct stone costs more than 2× a 1ct stone of equal quality.

Mohs Hardness Scale

Ranks minerals 1–10 by scratch resistance. Each mineral can scratch those below it. The scale is relative, not linear — diamond (10) is ~4× harder than corundum (9).

Optical Properties

Refractive Index (RI)

How much light bends entering the stone. Measured with a refractometer. Diamond: 2.42, Quartz: 1.54. Key diagnostic tool.

Dispersion

Splitting white light into spectral colors — the "fire" in a gemstone. Diamond's dispersion (0.044) creates its famous rainbow flashes.

Pleochroism

Showing different colors when viewed from different crystal directions. Tanzanite displays blue, violet, and burgundy depending on axis.

Luster

Quality of light reflected from the surface. Types: adamantine (diamond), vitreous (quartz), waxy, pearly, silky, metallic.

Fluorescence

Emission of visible light under UV radiation. ~25–35% of diamonds fluoresce blue under long-wave UV. Useful for identification.

Specific Gravity

Density relative to water. Measured by hydrostatic weighing. Helps distinguish look-alikes — glass (SG ~2.5) vs. sapphire (SG 4.0).

Chapter 3

Gem Identification

A systematic process of observation and measurement to determine a gemstone's identity and detect treatments or synthetics.

1

Visual Inspection

Observe color, transparency, luster, and any visible inclusions with the naked eye. Note the cut style and overall appearance. First impressions narrow the possibilities.

2

10× Loupe Examination

Inspect under magnification for inclusions, growth patterns, surface blemishes, and doubling of back facets (birefringence). Inclusions are often diagnostic — silk in sapphire, horsetails in demantoid garnet.

3

Refractive Index Test

Place the stone on a refractometer with contact liquid. Read the RI value(s). Single reading = isotropic (cubic system). Double reading = anisotropic. Compare against known RI tables.

4

Specific Gravity Test

Weigh in air, then immersed in water. SG = weight in air ÷ (weight in air − weight in water). Separates gems with similar appearance but different density.

5

Spectroscope Analysis

View the absorption spectrum. Each gem absorbs specific wavelengths — chromium lines in emerald (683nm, 680nm), iron bands in peridot. Distinguishes natural from synthetic.

6

Advanced Testing

UV fluorescence, polariscope (optic character), dichroscope (pleochroism), Chelsea filter, and microscopy. For conclusive results on difficult stones or treatment detection.

Chapter 5

Tools & Equipment

The essential instruments used in gemological examination and identification.

10× Loupe

Triplet lens for 10× magnification. Industry standard for grading clarity. Corrected for chromatic and spherical aberration. Essential first tool.

Refractometer

Measures refractive index using total internal reflection. Requires contact liquid (RI 1.81). Range: 1.30–1.81. Single most important identification instrument.

Spectroscope

Displays absorption spectrum of light passing through a gem. Prism type offers better resolution; diffraction grating type is easier to read.

Polariscope

Two polarizing filters determine optic character: singly refractive (SR), doubly refractive (DR), or aggregate (AGG). Also detects strain in SR stones.

Dichroscope

Shows pleochroic colors side by side through a calcite rhomb. Quick test to separate SR from DR stones and confirm gem identity.

UV Lamp

Long-wave (365nm) and short-wave (254nm) UV. Tests fluorescence and phosphorescence. Helps separate natural, synthetic, and treated stones.
